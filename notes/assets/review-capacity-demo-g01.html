01 / Define the boundary

Follow work before and after admission

Scroll figures sideways to inspect their labels on a narrow screen.

Work in progress, or WIP, means tasks we have admitted but have not yet decided. It includes execution, waiting for review and review itself. Tasks waiting for admission sit outside the WIP limit. We still count them when we measure the whole system.

04 / Follow the full workload

Follow all 24 tasks to a decision

With four execution slots, the reviewer stays supplied after startup. WIP 3 also keeps it supplied here, while holding most waiting upstream. B and C have identical per-task decision ticks.

05 / Read the wait correctly

Check the time from arrival to decision

Across all 24 tasks, B and C both have median end-to-end time 58 ticks and p95 102 ticks.

Median review-queue wait changes from 44 to 4 ticks; median admission wait changes from 0 to 40 ticks.

These percentiles include all 24 tasks. The median is the middle of the ordered values; p95 describes the upper end of the distribution. Component percentiles cannot generally be added, and measuring only tasks that finish early leaves out the slower work.

06 / Connect to systems thinking

Connect the queues to stocks and flows

A queue is a stock: work that has accumulated. Arrivals, admission and review are flows that move work into or out of those stocks. The WIP cap creates a balancing rule: when all places are occupied, admission waits until a decision releases room.

Observe only the review queue and C looks much faster. Include admission waiting and decisions, and the tradeoff becomes visible.

This is our interpretation of the teaching model. The experiment is not copied from Thinking in Systems, and it does not measure a real team.

07 / Challenge the explanation

Change one assumption and compare again

1. Change only C's WIP cap from 3 to 2. Does the reviewer become starved, and does full-drain time increase?

2. Change review service from 6 to 3 ticks for every policy. Does the limiting stage move toward execution?

3. Before applying this to actual agents, define quality, rework, failures and permission-release semantics.

Questions 1 and 2 use existing scenario fields. Question 3 requires a separately designed runtime experiment. This page replays one fixed scenario; it does not run interventions.

Limits of the evidence

Every review accepts. Arrivals are finite and scheduled; service demand is uniform. There is no output quality, rework, fatigue, priority, failure, live model, real human approval or measured agent execution time.

No cost reduction or quality improvement was measured.
